Criticism of the Consumption Culture: By Watching a Movie

For some reason or another, much of my childhood remains a blur, almost like looking through the windowpane of a car on a rainy afternoon. However, I can vividly recall how I spent a significant portion of my free time: sitting in front of my computer, watching animated movies pirated by my uncle.

Frozen, Rio, The Ice Age, Minions, Despicable Me, Brave, Madagascar - off the top of my head. It wouldn't be an exaggeration to say that many of the few fond memories I can still recall from my childhood involve watching animated movies.

There is one movie, and one character in particular, that left quite a enigmatic impression on me at that time. Only now as an adult, do I feel equipped to deconstruct it. The character is Anton Ego of the film Ratatouille. He is the most popular and the harshest food critic in all of France whose reviews make or break restaurants all across the country.

The climax of the movie comes when Ego (a very fitting name) is unexpectedly moved by a peasant dish "Ratatouille" prepared by the chef, a literal rat (yes, I acknowledge the absurdity of it). The movie does a wonderful job of depicting the gradual dismantling of his ego and the grand realization of his profession's mediocrity.

His subsequent monologue captures this far better than I ever could:

In many ways, the work of a critic is easy. We risk very little, yet enjoy a position over those who offer up their work and their selves to our judgment. We thrive on negative criticism, which is fun to write and to read. But the bitter truth we critics must face, is that in the grand scheme of things, the average piece of junk is probably more meaningful than our criticism designating it so.

This is, in essence, a criticism of his own criticism - of his work as a critic - but there is a subtle, broader point being made here: The average piece of junk is probably more meaningful than our criticism designating it so. In the era of consumerist culture, I'm afraid perhaps one of the greatest consequence has been the definition of our identities as what we consume rather than what we create.

We as a society have become accustomed to presenting ourselves through the things we consume: a cinephile, a swiftie, a bookworm. But do you really think that what you consume, defines you better than what you create or can create?

Show me your catalogue of the top 100 movies on your quirky Letterboxd account, but I would understand you more by watching a five-minute monologue of you talking about your day. You can buy a 1000 dollars table that matches the aesthetics of your room but I bet if you created your own table from hand, even the slightest deformities in the wood would still be more meaningful than your table from IKEA. Why? The deformities define you, and there is no greater feeling than recognizing a piece of yourself in something you've created.

I find this relationship between consumption and creation particularly evident in one of my hobbies: poetry. I love reading poetry, especially ghazals by Ahmad Faraz and Faiz Ahmad Faiz. Here's one of my favorite ones:

अब के हम बिछड़े तो शायद कभी ख़्वाबों में मिलें
जिस तरह सूखे हुए फूल किताबों में मिलें

Yet, I enjoy writing poetry just as much. None of my writings come anywhere close to the brilliance of Faraz's, but that hardly diminishes the joy I derive from writing them. In creating something - be it a poem, a song (a cover vaye pani), a dish you make off of Youtube, you inevitably create a little more of yourself. The act of creation is the act of creating yourself, no matter the source.

On a similar note, I am reminded of this meme that I came across, which perfectly describes how your consumerist identity prevents you from developing your own individuality. When your entire personality revolves around the things you consume, you risk coming across as a rather shallow person, someone whose identity can be summed up by their favourite Taylor Swift song, their favorite Office quotes, or their Letterboxd top four. These things might tell me what you enjoy, but how much do they actually tell me about you? What are your own thoughts, ideas, experiences, and creations? What have you brought into this world that is uniquely yours?

What I want you, the reader, to take away from this essay is: Don't let consumption consume you. And ask yourself this once: Does what you consume genuinely help you connect with your friends, with society, and, more importantly, with yourself?
